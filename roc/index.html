A Visual Introduction to Evaluating AI Online Week #1 · The ROC Curve & AUC
Before you start · a 2-minute walkthrough

Your AI claims 92% accuracy. Is that good enough?

Imagine your company runs breast-cancer screening, and you have built an AI that reads each mammogram and flags who might have cancer. It claims 92% accuracy. Before you rely on it, you have to judge it yourself — and to judge any AI, you first need to know how it can go wrong.

So, how many types of mistakes can this AI make?
Exactly — two. The AI can raise a false alarm (flag a healthy patient), or it can miss a real cancer. The two are not equally costly — and telling them apart is what this walkthrough is about. Next, we build the tools to see them: the confusion matrix, the ROC curve, and AUC.
Step 1 of 7

The setup: 10 patients, one score each

Suppose 10 patients come in for screening. In truth 4 have cancer and 6 do not, but you don't know which. The AI reads each mammogram and returns one risk score from 0 to 1 — not a yes or no, just the score.

Ideally the cancer patients would score high and the healthy ones low. In practice the two groups overlap, as the dots above show.

Step 2 of 7

Draw a line: set the threshold

You now have a score for every patient, but a score by itself does not tell you who has cancer. To decide, you draw a line, called the threshold. Patients at or above the line are called cancer; those below it are called clear. There is no single right place for the line, and moving it changes who gets called back.

Step 3 of 7

The two types of mistakes

Remember the question we opened with — how many types of mistakes can the AI make? Now that a threshold is set, here they are, and there are two. A patient who has no cancer but scores above the threshold is flagged as high risk, a False Positive or false alarm. A patient who has cancer but scores below the threshold is called clear, a False Negative or missed cancer. The missed cancer is the more serious mistake; a false alarm leads to an unnecessary biopsy and anxiety.

Step 4 of 7

The confusion matrix

Put the two mistakes together with the two correct outcomes, True Negative and True Positive, and you have the confusion matrix. This confusion matrix clearly depends on the threshold: move the threshold and all four counts change.

Step 5 of 7

The two errors have names: Type 1 and Type 2

Type 1 error = a False Positive: a false alarm, a healthy patient flagged as cancer.
Type 2 error = a False Negative: a miss, a real cancer called clear.

Now the trade-off. Set the threshold very high and you reduce Type 1 errors, the false alarms, but increase Type 2 errors, the missed cancers. Set it low and the reverse happens. Lowering one raises the other — and in screening, the Type 2 miss is the one that can cost a life.

On the confusion matrix, the False Positive sits top-right (Type 1) and the False Negative bottom-left (Type 2) — a handy way to keep the two straight.
Step 6 of 7

Every threshold gives a different confusion matrix

The confusion matrix depends on the threshold. Watch below: as the threshold slides from 1 down to 0, the four counts keep changing. Every setting gives a different matrix — so no single threshold is "the model." To judge the whole model, we need to look across all thresholds at once.

Step 7 of 7

The ROC curve, and its area (AUC)

Turn each threshold into a point: the True Positive Rate (cancers caught) up the side, the False Positive Rate (healthy patients flagged) across the bottom. Sweep every threshold and the points trace the ROC curve above. The area under it, the AUC, is one number for the whole curve: 0.5 is a coin flip (the diagonal), 1.0 is perfect (hugs the top-left). Because AUC does not depend on any single threshold, it judges the model itself — mammography AI models score roughly 0.61 to 0.70.

A stronger model bows further toward the top-left, and its AUC is higher.

So the order is: first pick the model by comparing ROC curves and AUC, then set that model's threshold based on which mistake is costlier. Choosing the model and choosing the threshold are two separate decisions.

Why this matters to a leader: the two errors are not equal, so put a real cost on each — the dollars and the human cost of a missed cancer versus a false alarm. That cost is what tells you where to set the threshold. The same curve can also reveal fairness gaps, where a model that looks strong overall performs near-randomly for a particular subgroup.
